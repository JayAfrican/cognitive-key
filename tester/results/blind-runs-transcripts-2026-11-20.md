### Reply

The sentence presents two negative judgements as though they were objective properties of a person. But it supplies almost none of the information needed to establish either judgement.

The central problem is that it turns an interpretation of behaviour into a description of identity.

## 1. What was each judgement measured against?

There are two explicit judgement terms and one implied judgement.

| Wording | What would need to be established? | What the sentence supplies |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Inappropriate" | What standard defines an appropriate level of independence? | No comparison named. |
| "Level" | What is the expected or required degree of independence? | No comparison named. |
| "Inflexible" | What degree of adaptation is expected, and under what circumstances? | No comparison named. |

For each judgement, the missing information is:

- Measured against what? A developmental expectation, workplace standard, task requirement, professional opinion, peer comparison, or something else?

- Measured by whom? A teacher, manager, clinician, family member, assessor, or report writer?

- Over what period? One incident, several observations, a term, a year, or an ongoing pattern?

- On what record? Direct observation, recorded incidents, assessment results, documented outcomes, or somebody else's account?

None of these is identified.

Consequently, the reader cannot distinguish an evidence-based assessment from an individual's preference or interpretation.

## 2. What is each claim a property of?

| Claim | What it is presented as | What it might actually be a property of |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "An inappropriate level of independence" | The person | The task, the support available, the setting, the relationship, or the writer's expectations |
| "Inflexible when routines change" | The person | The person's response at a particular moment, the nature of the change, the predictability of the setting, or the relationship with whoever introduced the change |

The distinction matters.

For example, a person who does not independently begin a task might be:

- Uncertain about what is expected.

- Waiting for information.

- Following previously established instructions.

- Unable to access the necessary resources.

- Choosing not to proceed without clarification.

Calling this an inappropriate level of independence makes all those possibilities disappear.

Similarly, difficulty responding to a changed routine might reflect the speed, scale, communication, or consequences of the change rather than a general characteristic of the person.

The sentence attributes both claims to the person without establishing that the person is where either problem resides.

## 3. How many separate things is this sentence describing?

There are two explicit behavioural claims:

1. Something about the person's independence.

2. Something about the person's response to changes in routine.

However, each claim contains additional elements.

First claim:

- Independence: a description of how much the person does without assistance.

- Inappropriateness: an evaluation of that amount against an unstated standard.

Second claim:

- A change in routine.

- A response to that change.

- An evaluation of that response as inflexible.

There is also a possible conflation within the second claim.

Not adapting immediately is not necessarily the same as being unable to adapt. Nor is requesting an explanation, expressing distress, or needing additional preparation necessarily evidence of inflexibility.

There is also a possible conflation between the two claims.

A person who seeks assistance may be described as insufficiently independent in one situation, while a person who independently maintains an established routine may be described as inflexible in another.

The two descriptions could therefore penalise opposite behaviours.

The sentence does not establish whether these are independent patterns, connected responses, or two interpretations of the same underlying difficulty.

## 4. What does each behaviour cost, and what does the wording cost?

This question needs to distinguish the actual cost of the behaviour from the potential cost of how it is documented.

### A. Independence

Potential costs to the person:

- If they act independently when support is needed, they may make mistakes, experience avoidable distress, or encounter safety risks.

- If they seek assistance when they could manage independently, they may experience reduced autonomy, unnecessary dependence, or fewer opportunities to develop skills.

- If their independence is judged against an inappropriate expectation, they may be denied reasonable support or criticised for exercising autonomy.

The sentence does not establish which of these, if any, is happening.

Cost of recording it this way:

The person may acquire a reputation for having poor judgement, being difficult to support, or failing to meet expectations.

The description may also lead subsequent readers to interpret ordinary requests for help as evidence of a personal deficit.

### B. Response to changes in routine

Potential costs to the person:

- Additional time and effort to understand and process a change.

- Anxiety, uncertainty, or distress.

- Loss of predictability or control.

- Conflict with people introducing changes.

- Reduced access to activities or opportunities if adaptation is treated as a condition of participation.

Cost of recording it this way:

A situational response can become a supposedly permanent personality characteristic.

The person may subsequently receive less preparation, less explanation, less flexibility, or less consideration because the report has already characterised them as inflexible.

### C. What would it cost the writer to be wrong?

Potentially:

- Loss of professional credibility.

- A misleading assessment or record.

- Poor decisions about support, intervention, access, or responsibility.

- Complaints or challenges to the report.

- Ethical or professional accountability.

- Harm to the relationship between the writer and the person being described.

There is an asymmetry here: the writer may have made a judgement in seconds, but the person may live with its consequences for years.

## 5. Who writes, reads, acts, and bears the consequences?

The sentence does not identify any of these people, so their identities cannot be established from the wording.

However, its likely information pathway is:

| Role | Possible person | Potential consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |

| Reads it | Supervisors, professionals, family members, decision-makers, or future assessors | May accept the judgement as established fact |

The report writer controls the description. Other people may control its application. The person described may bear the consequences without having participated in either decision.

## Rewrite

Original:

> Shows an inappropriate level of independence, and is inflexible when routines change.

Rewrite:

> The person's level of independence when completing [specified tasks] was below the level expected for those tasks, as assessed against [specified standard] by [specified assessor] during [specified observation period]. When [specified routines] changed, the person required [specified additional support or time] to adjust, compared with [specified expectation or documented baseline].

This retains the two most plausible intended meanings:

1. The writer believes the person is not demonstrating the expected degree of independent functioning.

2. The writer believes the person has difficulty adapting to changes in routine.

But it relocates the judgements from presumed personality characteristics to observable performance in identifiable circumstances.

Almost everything necessary to make the judgements accountable:

| Information needed | Supplied in original? |
| --- | --- |
| Which tasks independence refers to | No |
| Expected level of independence | No |
| Standard used for comparison | No |
| Identity or role of assessor | No |
| Observation period | No |
| Which routines changed | No |
| What the person actually did in response | No |
| What additional support or time was needed | No |
| Expected response or documented baseline | No |

The rewrite deliberately uses placeholders rather than pretending these facts are known.

There is one further distinction worth preserving: needing support is not itself evidence of inappropriate dependence, and needing time to adjust is not itself evidence of inflexibility.

A defensible report would need to show why either behaviour represents a problem in the relevant context, rather than assuming that difference from an unstated expectation is a problem.

The underlying issue: The original sentence tells the reader how to judge the person before telling them what the person actually did, what was expected, or why that expectation was appropriate.

It makes the claims difficult to challenge.

The sentence does not identify any of these people. They must therefore remain unknown.

There is an important power distinction here.

The writer controls the description. The reader may treat it as evidence. The decision-maker may act on it. The person described may have little control over any of those stages, yet may experience the greatest consequences.

That makes the absence of evidence particularly significant.

A sentence in a report is not merely a description. It can become part of the evidential basis for subsequent decisions about a person's competence, reliability, support needs or potential.

Original:

> "Struggles to prioritise and frequently misses the point of tasks, though clearly capable when engaged."

The original does not supply enough information to produce a fully evidence-based rewrite. The following details have therefore had to be introduced or assumed:

| Introduced detail | Why it was needed |

| Intended outcomes | To make "missing the point" more precise and assessable. |
| Priorities and outcomes specified in instructions | To provide a comparison standard. |

| Ability to meet relevant task requirements | To specify what capability means in practice. |
| On occasions | To avoid implying that capability is universal or guaranteed. |

There is one further qualification: even this rewrite does not establish who observed the behaviour, how many occasions were involved, what period was covered, or what records support the assessment.

Those would still need to be supplied.

### The fundamental distinction

The original sentence makes a claim about what kind of person someone is.

The rewrite attempts to make claims about what someone did, in relation to what standard, under what conditions.

That is the essential difference between a character judgement and an accountable assessment.

A report should not require the person being described to disprove an undocumented impression. The writer should be able to identify the observations, comparison standards and records that justify the conclusion.

## 1. What was each judgement measured against?

The sentence contains two explicit judgements and one implied comparative judgement.

| Wording | What was it measured against? | By whom? | Over what period? | On what record? |

The word better is particularly important: it implies a comparison has been made, but the sentence does not establish who made it or what evidence supports it.

## 2. What is each claim a property of?

- "Presents as guarded" — Grammatically, this describes how the person appears to an observer. It may be a property of a particular moment, interaction or relationship, but it is written as though it describes the person more generally.

- "Can be difficult to build rapport with" — This is potentially a property of a relationship between the person and another individual. It is not necessarily a property of the person alone. Rapport is jointly produced.

- "Responds better to structured settings" — This is a comparative claim about the interaction between the person and the setting. It suggests that environmental structure influences their responses.

There is also an unacknowledged fourth possibility: the writer's interpretation. The sentence tells us how the writer has characterised what they observed, not simply what happened.

## 3. How many separate things is this sentence describing?

I would identify three separate claims:

1. The person displays behaviour interpreted as guarded.

2. Establishing rapport with the person is experienced as difficult.

3. The person's responses differ according to the degree of structure in the setting.

There are two important compressions.

First: two things stated as one.

"Guarded" and "difficult to build rapport with" are presented as though they are closely connected.

But they are not interchangeable.

Someone might:

- Appear reserved but establish strong relationships over time.

- Be open and communicative but have difficulty trusting a particular professional.

- Be guarded in one environment and comfortable in another.

The sentence risks treating an observer's difficulty establishing rapport as evidence of a characteristic belonging to the person.

Second: one thing potentially stated as two.

"Guarded" and "responds better to structured settings" might describe two manifestations of the same underlying contextual pattern: the person communicates or participates differently depending on how predictable and organised an interaction feels.

Without examples, we cannot establish whether these are independent observations or different interpretations of the same behaviour.

## 4. What does each behaviour cost the person, and what does recording it this way cost them?

Appearing guarded

- Opportunities to have their needs understood.

- Time and effort spent establishing trust.

- Access to support if professionals interpret reserve as resistance.

- The ability to communicate at their own pace without being negatively characterised.

The sentence does not establish which explanation applies.

Difficulty building rapport

The person may experience:

- Repeated attempts at interaction that feel uncomfortable.

- Pressure to disclose information before feeling safe.

- Misunderstanding or frustration.

- A relationship in which responsibility for connection is placed disproportionately on them.

But the cost may also belong to the professional, the relationship or the circumstances. The wording obscures that distribution.

Responding better to structured settings

This may not be a difficulty at all.

Structure might reduce uncertainty, clarify expectations, make interactions more predictable or enable participation.

If so, the behaviour is potentially an indication of what helps the person engage, rather than evidence of a deficit.

### What does writing it this way cost the person?

The main cost is that a contextual observation can become a portable character judgement.

Once written into a report, "guarded" and "difficult to build rapport with" can:

- Influence how subsequent professionals approach the person.

- Lower expectations of cooperation or engagement.

- Encourage future observers to interpret ambiguous behaviour negatively.

- Become repeated in subsequent reports as though independently established.

- Shift responsibility for relationship difficulties from the interaction to the individual.

There is also an asymmetry: the writer's interpretation can become part of the person's record, while the person's own explanation may not appear anywhere.

### What would it cost the writer to be wrong?

If the writer incorrectly attributes a relationship or environmental difficulty to the person, they risk:

- Misrepresenting the person's behaviour.

- Undermining professional trust.

- Influencing decisions on an inaccurate basis.

- Creating a self-reinforcing account in which later professionals approach the person defensively and then interpret their response as confirmation.

The writer also risks overlooking something actionable: changing the structure, pace or conditions of an interaction might improve engagement.

The consequences of error are therefore not necessarily symmetrical. The writer makes the interpretation; the person may live with its consequences.

## 5. Who wrote it, who reads it, who acts on it, and who bears the consequences if it is wrong?

The sentence does not identify these people, so their actual identities cannot be established.

There may be secondary consequences for the writer, their organisation and other professionals.

But the distribution of power matters:

- The writer has the power to describe.

- The reader may have the power to interpret.

- The decision-maker may have the power to act.

- The person described may have to live with the result.

The person may have no equivalent opportunity to challenge the interpretation, provide context or correct the record.

## Rewriting

Original:

> Presents as guarded and can be difficult to build rapport with; responds better to structured settings.

Rewrite:

Writing

In the interactions observed by the report writer, the person appeared reserved, as assessed against the writer's expectations of engagement in those interactions, and establishing rapport was experienced by the writer as requiring additional effort. The writer observed more positive responses in structured settings than in less structured settings, using the person's observed engagement in those different settings as the basis for comparison.

### What had to be invented to write it?

The rewrite makes several things explicit that the original leaves unstated:

1. The observer: It assumes the report writer made the observations.

2. The meaning of guarded: It substitutes "reserved", assuming that the intended observable behaviour was limited or cautious engagement rather than hostility, distrust or something else.

3. The comparison for guardedness: It introduces the writer's expectations of engagement as the benchmark. The original supplied no benchmark.

4. The ownership of difficulty: It locates the difficulty in the writer's experience of establishing rapport rather than presenting it as an inherent property of the person.

5. The comparison for better: It assumes the comparison was between structured and less structured settings.

6. The meaning of better: It interprets better responses as more positive engagement. The original does not define the outcome being compared.

7. The evidential basis: It assumes there were observations across different settings.

An important limitation: These additions make the sentence more accountable, but they do not make the underlying claims verified. The report author would still need to supply actual observations, dates, examples and the criteria used.

The most consequential change is this:

The original makes the person the apparent location of the difficulty. The rewrite makes the observations, the relationship and the environment visible as separate parts of the account.

That distinction matters because a report should not quietly convert what happened between a person and their environment into what is wrong with the person.
