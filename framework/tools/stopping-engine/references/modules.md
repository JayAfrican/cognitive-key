# Stopping Engine — the four modules (steps 3, 5–8)

> Source: `stopping-engine.md` (v1, 2026-09-06; §6a added 2026-09-23). Copied verbatim for the plugin; the project document stays the master copy. If they disagree, the project document wins and this file gets refreshed.

## 3 · The four new modules

### Module A · Effect size and evidential weight

**The biggest gap for anything neuroscience- or health-adjacent.** Most bad claims in that space are not logically malformed — they are a real finding with a tiny effect, dressed as a life-changing one.

1. **Is an effect size reported at all?** If a claim cites research and no magnitude appears anywhere in the chain, **that absence is the finding.** Report it and stop.
2. **What is the number, and would a person notice it?** State d, r or the odds ratio in plain terms. Benchmarks are field-dependent — Funder & Ozer (2019) argue r ≈ .10 is typical and r ≈ .20 large in social and personality work, which is far below what the word "large" implies to a lay reader. **Say the number; do not say "significant."**
3. **Precision.** Sample size and confidence interval. A wide interval that includes zero, or includes both trivial and dramatic values, means the study has not narrowed the question.
4. **Replication status.** Has it replicated *directly*, not just conceptually? Conceptual replication with a different measure is weak evidence and is routinely reported as strong.
5. **The p-value trap.** p < .05 says the result is unlikely under the null. It says **nothing** about magnitude, importance, or whether the effect exists in the population.
6. **Single-study rule.** One study is a hypothesis. Treat it as such regardless of the journal.

> **Stop condition:** no effect size anywhere in the chain, or a single unreplicated study carrying a general claim → **OVERSTATED** at minimum.

### Module B · Causal inference

1. **Design.** Observational or experimental? Randomised? If observational, causal language in the claim is already an overstep by the claimant or the coverage.
2. **Direction.** Could B cause A? State the reverse explicitly and ask whether it was ruled out. *(Meditators are calmer — or calm people meditate.)*
3. **Confounding.** Name a third thing that plausibly causes both. If you can name one in under a minute and the study did not address it, say so.
4. **Selection and collider effects.** Who is in the sample and how did they get there?
5. **Bradford Hill, used honestly.** Strength, consistency, temporality, dose-response, plausibility, coherence, experiment. **These are heuristics for weighing, not a proof procedure**, and Hill said so.

> **Stop condition:** observational design carrying a causal claim, with an unaddressed nameable confounder → **UNSUPPORTED** as stated.

### Module C · Citation integrity

**This corpus discovered this pattern empirically before it had a name** — the biofield excerpt anchoring on a true claim (the heart is the strongest bioelectric generator) and carrying a false payload downstream on it.

1. **Read the actual source.** Does the cited paper say what the sentence it is attached to says? This single check kills more claims than any other and almost nobody runs it.
2. **Chain length.** How many hops from the claim to primary data? Claim → article → review → primary. **Every hop is a place meaning changes.**
3. **Load-bearing or decorative?** Is the citation supporting the specific assertion, or is it establishing that the topic exists?
4. **Citation distortion.** Greenberg (*BMJ*, 2009) traced how a network of citations converted an unsupported claim about amyloid-β into an established one purely through repetition. **Belief in a claim can be manufactured by citation traffic alone.**
5. **The laundering shape:** a true anchor citation carrying an untrue conclusion. Look for the point in the sentence where the citation stops covering the text.

> **Stop condition:** the source does not support the sentence → **UNSUPPORTED**, and note the distortion rather than only the error.

### Module D · Mechanism plausibility

**Generalised from the person-to-person field-coupling run (Inversion Log entry 27), which is the corpus's cleanest worked example.**

1. **Is a mechanism proposed at all**, or is it a correlation with a story attached?
2. **Enumerate what would have to be true.** Not "is it plausible" — list the conditions as a numbered set. Entry 27 produced seven for field coupling; four failed.
3. **Check each against a hard constraint:** conservation laws, thermal noise at body temperature, inverse-cube falloff, signal against the receiver's own background, ambient noise floor.
4. **Apply the three fates.** If a failing condition can be named as a *missing instrument or mechanism* → **shelved**. If nothing nameable would rescue it → **refuted**. This is the step that separates continental drift from the biofield.
5. **§4c interlock, mandatory here:** detectability is set by the best instrument ever built; efficacy is set by the receiver actually present. **State both thresholds in the same units.**

> **Stop condition:** any condition fails on physics no future instrument changes → **REFUTED**, not shelved.
